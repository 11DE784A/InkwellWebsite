In 1912, as the Titanic plunged into the Atlantic, it took with it what was
arguably the world's most luxurious book; a jewel-encrusted edition of Omar Al
Khayyam’s _Rubaiyat_, never to be recovered. The novelist Amin Maalouf took this
marginal historical fact, twisted it, and ran with it. In _Samarkand_ (his
novel), he assumes that the sunken book was Khayyam’s personal notebook, and
proceeds to imagine its remarkable life; beginning in the legendary
11th century city Samarkand and ending, centuries later, at the bottom of the
sea.  

Maalouf’s story starts in Samarkand, where Omar Al Khayyam arrives in the city;
quickly, his notoriety drives him to the most influential elites of the
kingdom. It was during his stay in Samarkand that he wrote his famous _Risala
fī’l-barahin ˓ala masa’il al-jabr wa’l-muqabala_ (A letter on proofs in
problems of algebra and inequalities), in which he solves a variety of cubic
polynomials using geometric methods that reduce the problem to intersections of
conics, i.e., degree two equations. In this part, Khayyam also meets other
iconic personalities of the history of Islamic civilisation, such as Nizam al
Mulk, a Saljuk Vizir, known for being a clever tactician and wise politician;
he is also famous for his book _Siyasat Nameh_, which translates to
'The Book of Politics', the “oriental” equivalent of Machiavelli's _The Prince_.
Amin Maalouf goes a bit further in comparing the two classics, saying: 

> This was a remarkable work, the Muslim world’s equivalent of Machiavelli’s _The
> Prince_, which was to appear in the West four centuries later with one crucial
> difference. _The Prince_ is the work of a man disappointed by politics and
> thwarted from having any power, while the _Siyasset Nameh_ is the fruit of the
> irreplaceable experience of an empire builder.

Another historical character that Khayyam meets is Hassan Al Sabbah, the
founder of the mythical order of Assassins (“Hashashin”), probably the most
powerful criminal organisation in history. In a very genius literary game,
involving Khayyam's _Rubaiyat_, the author jumps to the end of the 19th century,
where historical figures such as Djamel Eddine Al Afghani, a very influential
Islamic theologian and reformist, and Howard Baskerville, a young American
teacher. The latter was killed during the Persian Constitutional Revolution
while trying to break the siege of Tabriz (1908--1909).

Maalouf’s novel contains even more historical anecdotes that we will not expand
here to avoid undesirable spoilers. This novel, besides being very fun and
thrilling, is also a book about  some oriental history pedagogically written by
an oriental author, who is very knowledgeable about the Islamo--oriental
literature. You should definitely try it!
