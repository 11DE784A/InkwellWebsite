The concept of a "permanent residence" is foreign to me, as I have never had
one. As an international student from Pakistan, my only reference point has
been my home country. I often find it difficult to respond when people ask, "Do
you plan to return and settle there after finishing your studies?" Of course, I
always make up an answer. But the real answer is more complicated than they
might expect, entangled with visa policies, academic opportunities, and a soul
that has learned to be at home everywhere and nowhere at once.  

Being Pakistani has given me an unexpected expertise: I have become something
of a visa connoisseur. Over the past few years, I have obtained visas for more
countries than a younger I could imagine. Each relocation came with its own
bureaucratic odyssey: stacks of forms, bank statements, medical certificates,
letters of invitation. In another life, I might have made a career out of this
knowledge. Instead, it has simply become the unglamorous infrastructure of my
educational journey.  

Every time I moved to a different city or country, the tangible parts of my
life could be neatly packed into suitcases and boxes or, in my case, into a
single backpack alongside a well-worn passport. The body moves easily enough.
The harder part is that pieces of yourself stay behind: with childhood friends,
familiar streets, favourite restaurants, conversations that can never quite be
recreated somewhere else. 

I have never known what it feels like to have a place I could unquestionably
call home. My childhood became a sequence of departures. At six, my family
moved to another province; at eleven, we returned. At thirteen, we moved again.
At eighteen, university carried me to yet another city. Each move meant
beginning again, learning new streets, making new friends, leaving them behind.
The pattern continued, and later, at eighteen, I ventured to a new city for
university. The next four years became a cycle of arrivals and departures.
Until, at twenty-two, I made the bold decision to relocate to a foreign
country. This time, rebuilding my life from scratch proved exceptionally
difficult. Each passing day was tinged with profound longing for my mother and
friends, a constant reminder of the absence of a true home. However, my
transitory existence persisted; I found myself moving to yet another city
merely one year later.  

Over the next two years, I relocated every six months between Rome, Bremen, and
Belgrade before returning to Rome for my master's thesis. For a long time, I
thought these years had only taught me how to say goodbye. Looking back, I
realise they were teaching me something else. It occurred to me, gradually,
that my constant relocation was not merely a curse; it was also the
architecture of international academia itself. The pursuit of knowledge across
borders is built on movement. Each city offered me not just displacement, but
transformation. In Rome, I navigated the bureaucratic chaos and learned to
adapt with it. In Bremen, I discovered the importance of community among fellow
migrants. In Belgrade, I experienced how different cultural contexts shape the
same discipline in vastly different ways. These weren't just moves; they were
educations in themselves. What strikes me most, looking back, is how each
relocation forced me to build friendships from scratch and, in doing so, I
discovered something valuable: the capacity to connect deeply across
differences. My closest friends are now scattered across continents, each
bringing their own cultural perspective, language, and way of seeing the world.
Through them, I have learned to appreciate cultures I might never have
understood otherwise, to think beyond the narrow confines of my own background,
to see problems and possibilities through multiple lenses simultaneously.  

This mobility, which once felt like a burden, has broadened my horizons in ways
I couldn't have anticipated. I have internalised that there is no single
correct way to live, no singular "right" culture. I have learned resilience,
adaptability, and the surprising intimacy that comes from shared vulnerability
with strangers who become family. Eventually, I returned to Trieste to pursue
my PhD, and for the first time, the relocation felt less like running away and
more like choosing a place that holds promise for the next chapter.  

Throughout my journey across different cities, I have cherished friendships
only to watch many fade into distant memories, but I have also learned that
distance does not erase connection. The concept of "home" eludes me, lacking a
definitive definition in my personal dictionary. And yet, perhaps I have
discovered something else: a home that is not fixed to a place, but rather
woven into the relationships I have built and the person I have become through
constant movement. Instead, I wonder what the next city will teach me, what
friendships I will build, what version of myself I might become. Perhaps the
perpetual motion that has defined my life is not a failure to find home. It is,
itself, a home.
