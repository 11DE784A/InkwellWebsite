Young girls going to the river for water or flower picking in the woods – and
falling asleep while taking a break – or young men pasturing goats or meeting
in the central square to discuss village matters – Bulgarian folklore is mostly
a light, playful depiction of everyday scenes in Bulgarian village life. Most
of what survives in written or recorded form dates to the 19th and early 20th
centuries. This coincides with the Bulgarian National Revival (Възраждане),
when Bulgarian intellectuals started systematically writing down folk songs,
partly as a nation-building project – proving Bulgaria had a rich indigenous
culture distinct from Ottoman and Greek influence. So, although the
documentation happened in the 19th and the 20th century, the material itself is
generally understood to be much older – some of it plausibly pre-Christian in
origin, transmitted orally for centuries before anyone wrote it down. 

## Whatever happens in a Bulgarian village stays in a Bulgarian village 

While much of the folklore depicts innocent village scenes or sometimes praise
outlaws who fought off Ottoman attacks called _haiduks_ (masc.) and _haidutkis_
(fem.) (see [Sirma Voyvoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirma_Voyvoda) for an
example of the latter), some Bulgarian folk songs get surprisingly raunchy ---
and it's not an accident. 

An example is “Ела, моме, ела, душо“ (transliterated “Ela, mome, ela, dusho”) –
here is an _a cappella_ performance by my elementary school arts teacher, Milena
Karadjova:

I found the song surprising and quite revolutionary for being so explicit about
profoundly human and yet tabooized topics. And yet, “in a room full of
Bulgarian folk songs”, it could get away completely unnoticed, as it is also
entirely faithful to the tradition. It does not do away with those typical
light village life moments, it only squeezes in a playful and euphemistic
reference to a night of lovemaking, where the euphemism again draws on a
peculiar day-to-day rural life element. Uplifted by the simultaneous novelty,
flirtatiousness and lightness of this old love song, I thought it worthy of
international attention and translated it into English and Italian, trying to
maintain the rhythm and meter of the Bulgarian original. But before getting to
the lyrics of the song, let us try to trace the cultural history of sexual
themes in Bulgarian folklore. This journey through Bulgaria’s religious and
cultural anthropology and history will shed some light on why traditional
village culture, of all places, produced such openly sexual material. However,
the answer has, curiously and perhaps disappointingly, less to do with
conscious and “revolutionary” taboo-breaking as I initially thought and more to
do with farming, magic, and weddings. 

## Sexuality and the harvest in peasant cultures 

Old European peasant cultures – Bulgaria included – often linked human
sexuality to the fertility of the land. The logic was the following: if you
want the fields, the animals, and the newlyweds to be fruitful, you sing about
sex at the right ritual moments – sowing, harvest, weddings. This is an
instance of sympathetic magic: sing dirty, get a good crop and healthy babies. 
 

## Spiritual pest control 

In Bulgarian wedding tradition, you get this odd combination: a priest blesses the couple in church, and then, later the same day, the bridesmaids and best man sing genuinely filthy songs mocking and teasing the newlyweds. That's not disrespect, but quite the opposite: crude humor and sexual joking were believed to ward off the evil eye and curses aimed at the couple – obscenity becomes protection, not a scandal. 

## Christianity and paganism --- coexisting neighbors?  

Before Christianity, Bulgarian lands had Thracian and Slavic pagan fertility cults built around the farming calendar. When Christianity took over in the 9th century, it didn't stamp out these customs – it absorbed them, because they were baked into the yearly rhythm of village life (spring rites, harvest songs, wedding customs). Folklorists call this "double faith": church Christianity and pagan-rooted folk practice running side by side, the latter being, let’s say, tolerated rather than approved. 

## Enough background --- back to "Ela, mome, ela, dusho" 

Great to know all of this so far, but this song is not about harvest, a wedding
or anything related. It is about a girl inviting her neighbor, another young
woman, over for dinner, and receiving a “no” with a surprising excuse. The song
belongs to a lighter genre – romantic, teasing courtship songs rather than
heavy-duty fertility ritual. The “linguistic” magic warding off evil spirits or
ensuring a rich harvest has faded into flirtation. But “Ela, mome, ela, dusho”
could get away with being playfully suggestive precisely because older, more
ritual songs had already made frank talk about bodies and desire a normal part
of public singing – not something scandalous. In a sense, this song is way less
a revolution than it is yet another everyday-life song for its time. Maybe that
is why it seems so revolutionary and taboo-breaking to me now! Indeed, it was
19th – 20th century urban, more posh sensibilities that later found this
embarrassing, which is why a lot of collected folk material got quietly
"cleaned up" or left out of published anthologies. “A shame”, Milena Karadjova
told me once, “because those folk songs preserve something very deeply and
authentically human. Those Bulgarians, who were living in small, far-off
mountain villages, as protected as possible from the dominating Ottomans in the
lowlands, needed some kind of outlet in their otherwise quite isolated rural
life – and lovemaking for pleasure was readily available. What a human thing to
do.” 

Without further ado, I present to you “Ela, mome, ela, dusho” first in the
Bulgarian original and then in English and Italian translation, fruits of a
fun, creative August afternoon.
